Most people already have a skill someone would pay for. Maybe you're the friend who fixes spreadsheets, the one who plans every trip, or the person co-workers ask when their laptop acts up. What's usually missing isn't the skill. It's the **offer**: a clear, specific package that a stranger can understand in ten seconds and say yes to.

"I do bookkeeping" is a skill. "I'll clean up one month of receipts and set up a simple expense sheet for your food truck in one week" is an offer. This guide walks you through turning the first into the second, step by step. You don't need a website, a logo or a business plan to start. You need a page of notes and about an hour.

## Why "I can help with anything" doesn't sell

When you describe your skill broadly, you make the buyer do all the work. They have to figure out whether you can help with their problem, what you'd actually do, how long it takes and what it might cost. Most people won't bother. They move on to someone whose offer already answers those questions.

A good offer answers five questions up front:

1. **Who is it for?** One type of person or business, not "everyone."
2. **What problem does it solve?** In the buyer's words, not yours.
3. **What exactly do they get?** A short list of concrete deliverables.
4. **How long does it take?** A clear start-to-finish timeline.
5. **What does it cost?** A specific price, or a small set of options.

Everything below is about answering those five questions well.

## Step 1: List what you can actually do

Write down every skill you have that someone has thanked you for, asked you about twice, or that you could teach a beginner. Don't filter yet. Include work skills, hobby skills and "life admin" skills.

Then, next to each one, add a quick note:

- **Proof:** where you've done it before (a job, a family business, volunteer work, your own projects).
- **Enjoyment:** would you be happy doing this for a few hours a week?
- **Tools:** what you'd need that you don't already have.

Circle the two or three skills where you have some proof, you wouldn't mind the work, and you need little or nothing new to start. Those are your candidates.

## Step 2: Pick one person to help

This is the step most people skip, and it matters most. Choose one specific group of people who have the problem your skill solves. Good groups are easy to picture and easy to find.

- Too broad: "small businesses."
- Better: "local restaurants."
- Best for a first offer: "food trucks in my area that do their own books."

Ask yourself: where do these people already gather (online groups, local associations, markets, forums)? Could I talk to five of them this month? If the answer is no, pick a group you can actually reach.

## Step 3: Name the problem in their words

People don't buy "bookkeeping." They buy relief from a shoebox of receipts and a nagging worry about tax time. Write down the problem as your buyer would say it out loud. If you can, ask two or three people in your chosen group: "What's the most annoying part of [the area your skill covers]?" Write down their exact words. Those phrases will become your offer description and your pitch.

## Step 4: Package it into deliverables

Turn your skill into a short, finite list of things the buyer receives. Finite matters. "Ongoing help" is hard to price and hard to say yes to. "These four things, done by this date" is easy.

Aim for three to six deliverables, for example:

- A one-hour kickoff call
- A cleaned-up, categorized record of one month of expenses
- A simple spreadsheet they can keep using
- A 30-minute walkthrough on how to keep it current
- One week of email questions after delivery

Then give the package a plain name that says what it is, like "Food Truck Books Tune-Up." Clever names can wait. Clear names sell.

## Step 5: Set a timeline you can keep

Write down what happens on each day, from kickoff to handoff. Be honest about your available hours, and add a buffer. A timeline that feels a little slow to you but is reliable is better than a fast one you miss. Something like "kickoff on day 1, first draft by day 3, finished by day 7" is perfect for a first offer.

## Step 6: Choose a starting price (and write down why)

There's no magic formula, but there is a sensible process:

1. **Estimate your hours.** Add up the time for every deliverable, including calls and revisions.
2. **Look at what others charge.** Search for similar services in your area or online and note a low, middle and high price. Look at the scope, not just the number.
3. **Factor in your experience.** If you're newer, start at the lower end of the range and say so honestly. Raise prices as you gain experience and feedback.
4. **Pick a range, then one number.** Settle on a starting price you'd be comfortable defending, plus one optional add-on (like monthly upkeep).

Write down two sentences explaining your price. If a buyer asks, you'll have a calm, clear answer. Remember that a price is a starting point you test, not a prediction of what you'll earn. Some offers need several rounds of adjustment before they find buyers.

## Step 7: Write a one-line pitch and a short post

Your one-line pitch combines who, problem and result: "Behind on your food truck's books? I'll clean up a month of receipts and set you up with a simple system in one week."

Then write a short post or message of 60 to 110 words that expands on it: who it's for, what they get, the timeline, and an easy next step ("Message me if you'd like a hand"). Keep it honest and friendly. Skip hype, fake urgency and claims you can't back up.

## Step 8: Test it with real people

Share your pitch with five to ten people in your chosen group, or in one place they gather. Listen to the questions they ask, because those questions show you what's unclear. Adjust the name, the deliverables or the price, and try again. Getting no response at first is normal. It's information, not failure.

## A worked example: from "good with spreadsheets" to a clear offer

Here's how the steps play out for one person, Dana, who did the books for her family's restaurant for six years.

- **Skill:** bookkeeping and spreadsheet cleanup. Proof: six years at the family restaurant. Enjoys it: yes. New tools needed: none.
- **Who:** food truck owners in her area who do their own books.
- **Problem (their words):** "I've got a bag of receipts and no idea what I'm actually spending."
- **Offer name:** Food Truck Books Tune-Up.
- **Deliverables:** kickoff call; one month of receipts sorted and categorized; a simple expense spreadsheet; a 30-minute walkthrough; one week of email questions.
- **Timeline:** kickoff day 1, spreadsheet by day 3, walkthrough by day 7.
- **Starting price:** after comparing local and online bookkeeping clean-up services and estimating about six hours of work, Dana picks a starting price toward the lower end of what she found, because she's new to selling this as a service. She adds optional monthly upkeep as an add-on.
- **One-line pitch:** "Behind on your food truck's books? I'll sort a month of receipts and set you up with a simple system in one week."

Dana now has something she can post in a local food truck group today. She doesn't know yet whether anyone will buy it, and that's fine. She knows exactly what she's offering, which makes it much easier for the right person to say yes.

## Your offer checklist

Copy this list and check every line before you share your offer:

- ☐ I picked **one** skill I have real proof for.
- ☐ I picked **one** specific group of people I can actually reach.
- ☐ I wrote the problem in **their** words.
- ☐ My offer has a plain, descriptive **name**.
- ☐ It lists **3–6 concrete deliverables**, with nothing open-ended.
- ☐ It has a **timeline** I can keep, with a buffer.
- ☐ I chose a **starting price** and wrote two sentences explaining it.
- ☐ I have a **one-line pitch** and a **short post** (60–110 words).
- ☐ I know **where** I'll share it first and with **whom**.
- ☐ I've checked any **licenses, insurance or rules** that apply to this kind of work where I live.
